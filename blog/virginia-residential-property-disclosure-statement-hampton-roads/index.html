Virginia Residential Property Disclosure Statement: What Hampton Roads Buyers and Sellers Need to Know

A plain-English guide to Virginia's Residential Property Disclosure Statement for Hampton Roads home buyers and sellers, from Chris Jenkins of Better Homes and Gardens Real Estate.

By Chris Jenkins · Better Homes and Gardens Real Estate – Native American Group · Published November 14, 2026

Quick answer

Virginia uses a Residential Property Disclosure Statement, published by the Virginia Real Estate Board, that tells buyers to do their own due diligence rather than listing every defect. Hampton Roads buyers should read it before contract ratification and pair it with a full inspection and a flood-zone check. What Is the Virginia Residential Property Disclosure Statement?

When you buy or sell a home in Hampton Roads, one of the first documents you will see is the Residential Property Disclosure Statement. It comes from the Virginia Residential Property Disclosure Act (Va. Code § 55.1-700 et seq.), which governs the information owners must give prospective purchasers of residential property. The Virginia Real Estate Board, part of the state's Department of Professional and Occupational Regulation (DPOR), publishes the official form, and DPOR lists the current version as effective July 1, 2025.

The key thing to understand as a buyer is the headline on the form itself: it carries a required notice that the buyer must exercise necessary due diligence. In plain terms, Virginia leans toward "buyer beware." The statement does not work like a detailed home-condition checklist. It tells you what the seller is and is not making representations about, and it points you toward your own inspections and research.

When Does the Seller Have to Provide It?

According to the Northern Virginia Association of REALTORS®, the Act requires the seller to give the buyer a notification, on a form developed by the Real Estate Board, before the contract is ratified. The Act also places certain affirmative disclosure duties on sellers, and in each case the seller must have "actual knowledge" of the particular facts. Whether and when a buyer can terminate over a late disclosure depends on the timing, so ask your agent and, if needed, your attorney.

Special Disclosures Worth Knowing About

Beyond the standard statement, Virginia and federal law call out a few specific situations. Summaries of Virginia law note that sellers must tell buyers when a property sits near a military air installation, which matters across Hampton Roads because of NAS Oceana, NALF Fentress and other installations, and when a property was previously used to manufacture methamphetamine and was not properly cleaned. Federal law also requires disclosure of known lead-based paint hazards for homes built before 1978. If a home is near an installation, review the Navy's noise and accident-potential maps for that address yourself.

What Buyers in Chesapeake, Virginia Beach and Norfolk Should Do

  1. Read the statement and the acknowledgment before you sign a contract, and ask your agent to explain every line that is marked "no representations."
  2. Order a full home inspection, plus the specialty inspections that fit the property: termite and wood-destroying insect, sewer scope, radon, and crawl space or moisture checks on older homes and waterfront lots.
  3. Check flood zones and insurance early. Much of Hampton Roads sits in or near flood zones. Get the flood-zone determination and an insurance quote before your inspection period ends.
  4. If the home is in an association, review the association disclosure packet for dues, rules and special assessments.
  5. Use your agent. A Hampton Roads agent who has handled hundreds of closings will know which questions to ask for a given neighborhood, such as Mill Creek Harbor in Chesapeake.

What Sellers Should Do

If you are selling, complete the statement accurately and deliver it on time. Do not guess about things you do not know, and do not hide problems you do. "As is" language does not protect a seller who knowingly conceals a defect. Before listing, fix or disclose the issues you already know about, and consider a pre-listing inspection so there are fewer surprises after the contract.
